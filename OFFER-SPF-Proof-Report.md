## 0. What this document is

One offer, specified completely: what it is, who buys it, what they get, what it costs, how it is delivered, how integrity is protected, and exactly what to say to sell it.

It deliberately does **not** sell certification. Per the Operating Plan (Part B), The Clean Sheet has no operative certified standard and no surveillance capability yet, so it sells a **point-in-time Evidence Report** — a product that is honest today, generates revenue today, and builds the registry and methodology that a badged scheme will later stand on.

**The strategic bet in one line:** stop selling compliance to QA, start selling proof to marketing.

## 1. Why sunscreen, and why now

**The category has a trust vacuum with a price tag.** Independent SPF testing controversies over the last two years have done something unusual: they punished honest brands and dishonest brands equally, because consumers cannot tell them apart. A brand that tests properly, labels conservatively and holds a valid ISO 24444 report currently has **no way to say so credibly.** Their own lab report is not a public asset — it is a PDF from a lab nobody has heard of, and posting it invites more scrutiny, not less.

That is the gap. You are not selling "we will check if you are hiding something." You are selling **"you have nothing to hide and no way to prove it. We fix that."**

**Four reasons sunscreen is the right wedge:**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Bounded** | SPF/UVA/water-resistance claims have defined test methods and defined pass criteria. The verdict is defensible in a way "is this product clean?" never is. You can be right, and show why. |
| **Consequential** | An SPF claim is the only cosmetic claim in India where under-delivery causes actual harm. That makes it the one claim consumers, press and regulators actually chase. |
| **Contested in public** | The discourse already exists. You are not creating awareness, you are arriving into an argument that is already running. |
| **Competitive** | Once two sunscreen brands hold a proof page, the third has a problem. Certification models spread slowly; competitive proof spreads fast. |

**It also fits what you already have.** Your evaluation library already contains the Sun Verified module, LAB_REQUIREMENTS.md, CLAIMS_EVIDENCE_GUIDE.md and the Consumer Proof Page template. This offer is not new IP. It is your existing IP, cut down to one category and repriced to close.

## 2. The customer

### Ideal buyer profile

- **Company:** Indian founder-led skincare or suncare brand, ₹5 crore–₹150 crore annual revenue
- **Product:** at least one SPF SKU that is either the hero product or the next launch
- **Manufacturing:** third-party contract manufacturer (so the brand owns the claim but not the lab file — this is the pain)
- **Buyer:** Founder, or Head of Brand/Marketing. **Not** the QA or regulatory manager.
- **Budget line:** launch marketing or brand budget, not compliance

### Why the buyer is marketing, not QA

QA has no budget, no upside, and a strong incentive to say "we already have the report." Marketing has 20x the budget, is measured on differentiation, and is currently losing sleep over a category-wide credibility problem it cannot solve with more ad spend. Sell to the person who benefits from the *output*, not the person who is judged on the *input*.

### The seven buying triggers

Only approach brands showing one of these. Everyone else is a conversation, not a customer.

1. **Launching an SPF product** in the next 90 days
2. **A competitor's SPF claim was publicly challenged** in the last 60 days
3. **Their own claim was questioned** — Reddit, a dermatologist creator, a review, a customer complaint
4. **Marketplace or q-commerce friction** — listing flagged, claim copy rejected, takedown
5. **Retail or export expansion** — Nykaa/Tira onboarding, or EU/UK/UAE entry needing a dossier
6. **Reformulation or CM change** — the existing test report may no longer describe the shipping product
7. **Fundraise or acquisition diligence** — someone is about to read their claim file properly

### Disqualify fast

No SPF SKU · sub-₹2 crore revenue · white-label reseller with no formulation control · anyone who opens with "how much does the badge cost." That last one is buying a sticker, not proof, and they are the customers who will eventually destroy the mark.

## 4. The 12-point verification protocol

This is the methodology. It is what they are buying, it is what makes the report defensible, and it is what most brands cannot do internally — not because it is secret, but because it requires reading a lab report adversarially, which no one inside a brand is incentivised to do.

**1 · Sample identity and traceability.** Does the tested sample correspond to the shipping product? Formula code, version, batch, manufacture date, and whether any reformulation has occurred since the test. *In practice this is where a large share of findings appear: the report is genuine, but it describes an earlier formulation.*

**2 · Laboratory competence.** NABL or ISO/IEC 17025 accreditation, current at the date of testing, with the specific method inside the accredited scope. Report format consistent with the lab's template; anomalies flagged.

**3 · In-vivo SPF determination.** ISO 24444. Panel size and validity, subject phototype distribution, standard deviation and 95% confidence interval, and whether the labelled SPF is properly supported by the measured mean and its confidence interval — not rounded up to it.

**4 · UVA protection.** ISO 24442 (in vivo PPD) or ISO 24443 (in vitro UVA-PF). Verification of the PA rating derivation, the UVA-PF to SPF ratio, and critical wavelength where broad-spectrum is claimed.

**5 · Label value vs measured value.** Whether the number on the pack is the number the data supports, under the applicable labelling convention. *Confirm the current CDSCO/BIS position on sunscreen SPF labelling at the time of each engagement — this is an area of active regulatory movement and must not be quoted from memory.*

**6 · Water and sweat resistance.** ISO 16217 (water resistant) or ISO 18861 (very water resistant), retention threshold met, and — critically — whether the *claim wording* matches the *protocol run*. "Water resistant for 80 minutes" and "water resistant" are different claims requiring different tests. Absolute claims ("sweat-proof", "sunblock", "waterproof") are not permitted at all.

**7 · UV filter legality.** Every filter and its concentration against the Indian Cosmetics Rules 2020 and BIS listings, plus EU Annex VI where export is declared. Flags where a filter is permitted in India but restricted in a declared export market.

**8 · Photostability.** Whether the filter system holds under UV exposure — filter combinations, known incompatibilities, presence and adequacy of photostabilisers. A photo-unstable system delivers less protection in the sixth hour than in the first, and the SPF certificate will never show it.

**9 · Stability, packaging and preservation.** Stability data supporting the shelf life, packaging compatibility, and preservative efficacy (ISO 11930). SPF at end of shelf life is the number the consumer actually gets.

**10 · Claim language audit.** Every SPF-adjacent claim assessed against the Claims Evidence Guide: broad spectrum, blue-light protection, pollution protection, "dermatologically tested", "suitable for sensitive skin", "reef safe", "no white cast", "non-comedogenic", and any prohibited absolute or drug claim.

**11 · Statutory labelling.** Cosmetics Rules 2020 and Legal Metrology (Packaged Commodities) Rules: manufacturer name and address, manufacturing licence number, batch number, manufacture date, best-before, net quantity, ingredient declaration in descending order, directions and mandatory warnings.

**12 · Live marketing sweep.** What the brand's own assets actually say right now — website, pack, performance ads, marketplace and q-commerce listings, influencer briefs. Evidence held centrally is worthless if the ad running today says something the file does not support. *This is the finding brands react to most strongly, because it is visible, fixable and entirely theirs.*

## 6. Integrity protocol — the part that determines whether this becomes a business or a liability

The commercial trap in every certification model: the person who pays wants a pass. Address it explicitly, in writing, on day one, in the sales conversation itself. Handled well, it is a selling point.

**1. The fee buys the assessment, not the outcome.** Stated in the engagement letter, said out loud on the first call, printed on the invoice. *"You are paying us to look properly. You are not paying us for a particular answer, and no amount of money buys one."*

**2. Failure is private.** If a product does not meet the criteria, no public page is issued, no registry entry is created, and The Clean Sheet does not publish or disclose the failure. The brand receives a **Remediation Report** instead: what failed, why, and the specific route to fixing it. No refund — the work was done and the answer was delivered.

**3. Re-assessment at 50%** once remediation is complete, within 12 months.

**4. The badge is never the deliverable, and never sold separately.** It is a consequence of a result. Anyone who tries to buy the mark directly is refused. Write this into the price list so it is not a judgement call under pressure.

**5. Aggregate transparency.** Publish annually: number of SKUs assessed, number verified, number not verified, most common failure modes — with no brand named. This is what stops "verified" from meaning "paid". Without it, the mark is worth nothing within two years. With it, the mark is worth something precisely because the denominator is visible.

**6. Decision separation.** The person who sells does not decide. Every verdict is reviewed by a second assessor before communication. Record the conflict-of-interest declaration for each engagement. This is your ISO 17065 alignment made operational rather than aspirational, and at your current size it costs you one contractor reviewer per engagement.

**7. No margin on testing.** Lab costs pass through at cost with the invoice visible to the brand.

**8. Validity and voiding.** Verification is point-in-time, valid 12 months. It voids automatically on any change to formulation, manufacturing site, or the claims made. The brand agrees in the licence to notify within 30 days of any such change. Non-notification is grounds for immediate withdrawal and registry removal.

**9. Withdrawal.** Reserve, in writing, the right to withdraw verification and remove the registry entry if a market pull contradicts the original result or a licence condition is breached. Withdrawal is publishable; failure at initial assessment is not. The difference matters: one is a broken promise made to consumers, the other is a private diagnosis.

**10. Say the limits out loud on the proof page.** "This verification covers the claims listed on this page for the batch and formulation tested on [date]. It is not an assessment of the product's suitability for any individual, and it is not a government approval." A scheme that names its own limits is more credible, not less.

**Why it works:** it opens with their category problem rather than your product, it flatters honestly and specifically, it names the deliverable in one sentence, and it asks for a small thing.

### Discovery call — six questions, in order

1. "Which SPF SKU matters most to you commercially right now?"
2. "Who ran your SPF and UVA testing, and when?"
3. "Has the formulation changed at all since that test?" *(Silence here is the whole engagement.)*
4. "If a dermatologist creator posted tomorrow that your SPF 50 tests at 22, what happens over the following week?"
5. "Who would sign off on ₹95,000 for this, and what would they need to see?"
6. "What would have to be true for you to move on this within 30 days?"

Question 4 is the one that converts. Do not soften it, and do not fill the pause after it.

### The close

> "Based on what you've told me, I'd recommend the Proof Report on [SKU] — that's ₹75,000 under the founding cohort rate, plus lab costs at cost, roughly [X] for the tests you'd need. Four to six weeks. I'll send the engagement letter and payment link today. Shall I?"

Then stop talking. Do not add "no pressure." Do not add "let me know your thoughts." The silence is doing the work.

### Objection handling

**"We already have a lab report."**
> "Good — that puts you ahead of most. Two things though. First, your customer has never seen it and wouldn't know how to read it. Second, in most engagements I find the report is genuine but describes a formulation from before the last reformulation. Worth checking which one you're in."

**"₹95,000 is a lot."**
> "It's about a week of your performance media. The difference is you own this permanently and it works on every visitor rather than the ones you paid for that week. And if you'd rather start smaller, the ₹15,000 screen tells you where you're exposed and comes off the price if you go ahead."

**"What if we fail?"**
> "Then you find out privately from me, in a report that tells you exactly how to fix it — instead of publicly from a creator with 400,000 followers. Failures are never published. That's in the contract. Honestly, that's most of the value: you're buying the right to find out first."

**"Nobody has heard of The Clean Sheet."**
> "True, and that's why you're getting founding pricing and a permanent place on the register. But the report doesn't rest on my reputation — it rests on an ISO method, a NABL-accredited lab, and a product I bought off the shelf like any customer. You can check every part of it. That's the point."

**"Our contract manufacturer handles all this."**
> "They handle the manufacturing licence. The claim on the front of the pack is yours — you own it, you market it, and if it's challenged, you answer for it. Your CM won't be in that conversation."

**"Let's do it after launch."**
> "Launch is when the claim gets read most carefully — by creators, by competitors, by marketplace teams. And it's the one moment you can't fix the artwork. Two weeks before is the cheapest this ever gets."

**"Send me a proposal."**
> "I will — today. So it's actually useful, which SKU should I scope it to, and is there anyone else who should be on it?"

### Proposal — one page, always

Name of SKU · claims in scope · the 12 protocol points, listed · deliverables, listed · timeline with dates not durations · price with lab estimate shown separately · what happens if it doesn't pass · validity period · signature block and payment link. **One page.** A long proposal signals you are still selling; a one-pager signals the decision is small.

## 10. What to give up to make this work

**Scope.** No baby care, no serums, no cleansers for 90 days. One category, or you will never build enough repetition to get fast, and speed is the entire margin.

**The word "clean."** It has no defensible definition and it costs you exactly the science-literate audience most likely to amplify you. Use *verified*, *substantiated*, *independently tested*. The brand name can stay; the vocabulary of the product should not use it.

**The comprehensive standard, for now.** A standard is the output of assessing thirty products, not the input. Every paid engagement should add to a structured dataset — failure modes, lab quality, filter systems, claim patterns — and v1.0 should be written from what you actually found. That standard will be better than the one you would write in advance, and you will be able to defend every threshold in it because you will have seen the distribution.

**The idea that this must be certification.** The mission is that consumers can tell the difference between evidence and marketing. Certification is one form that could take. Advisory, media, data and retailer diligence are others. Staying attached to the form is the most likely way to lose the mission.
