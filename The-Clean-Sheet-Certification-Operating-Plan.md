Both are built on the retired scored and tiered model. The gate-based Product Standards Framework 2026 that replaces them is still Version 0.9, under validation. So the true status is: **standard in development, no version currently active for certification.**

Until Version 1.0 is validated and the decision and surveillance systems are running, The Clean Sheet does three things only:

1. Publishes a framework that is openly marked as under validation.
2. Runs public evidence reviews (public information only, clearly not certification).
3. Offers point-in-time evidence reports to brands (a paid product that needs no ongoing surveillance).

It does not issue badges, and it does not say "Apply for certification." Everything below follows from that.

## Part B. The scheme decision (make this before building more)

Everything downstream depends on two choices.

**Scheme type.** Adopt a Type 5 style model for any on-pack badge: initial product evaluation, a manufacturing or quality-system assessment, an independent review and decision, a licence to use the mark, periodic production or market sampling, and continuing surveillance. If you cannot yet deliver the surveillance, you do not put a continuing badge on the pack. You sell a point-in-time Evidence Report instead. This is the bridge that lets you earn revenue now without overclaiming.

**Accreditation posture (decided).** Start as an unaccredited private scheme with exact disclosure. Name NABCB accreditation, for a defined product-certification scope, as the stated roadmap, and keep partnering with a competent external certification body as an option if you later want a faster route to recognised status. The one rule that never bends: do not imply you are accredited until you are.

**The business reality the review understates:** continuing surveillance (market pulls, factory audits) is expensive per product and brutal at low volume. The Evidence Report is not a fallback, it is the right first product. It funds the operation and builds the registry while you grow toward a surveilled badge.

**Legal spine.** Decide the legal entity, get professional indemnity insurance, and put a limitation-of-liability line on every certificate and every report. This belongs next to the trademark work, not after it.

## Part D. Separate Reviews from Certification, in the operation not just the nav

The website separation is done. The operational separation is what actually protects impartiality:

Different people and decision authority for reviews versus certification. Separate records and commercial targets. No sales incentive tied to a negative review. A brand right-to-reply on both sides. A public correction and revision history. Independent appeals for certification decisions. This maps directly to gate G06 in your framework and to ISO 17065's impartiality requirement.

## Part E. The Version 1.0 validation protocol (the Founding Validation Cohort)

"Expert validation" needs to be a defined process, and this is the single highest-credibility thing you can do. Before Version 1.0 goes active:

1. Run eight to twelve confidential shadow assessments on real products.
2. Have at least two assessors independently assess the same products.
3. Measure whether they reach the same gate decisions, and record the disagreement rate.
4. Resolve every ambiguous decision rule the disagreements expose.
5. Validate each launch module with the relevant specialist.
6. Publish a thirty-day stakeholder consultation draft.
7. Publish an anonymised comment-and-response log.
8. Approve the final version through a documented authority.

Call these companies the Founding Validation Cohort. They do not get an active badge during the research stage. You already have the intake machinery for this: `PRE_ASSESSMENT_QUESTIONNAIRE.md`, `BRAND_DOSSIER_CHECKLIST.md`, `EVALUATION_GUIDE.md`, and two real dossiers (Bare Chemistry's pre-assessment form and the CodeSkin brand dossier). Rewrite the intake to the gate model and run those two as your first shadow assessments.

## Part G. Write the Scheme Manual (the private operating manual)

The public standard says what a product must meet. The Scheme Manual says how The Clean Sheet works. Cover: application and contract review, scope determination, an assessor competence matrix, laboratory acceptance and exceptions, sample selection and chain of custody, technical review and the certification decision, an impartiality risk register, subcontractor management, complaints and appeals, suspension and withdrawal and expiry, mark misuse and enforcement, and record retention and document security.

Build in the efficiency the review points to: you do not need three scientists for every ordinary SKU. A routine product gets one qualified assessor plus an independent reviewer and decision. The multidisciplinary panel is reserved for boundary cases and policy questions. ## Part K. Benchmarked against the major markets (and how to say it)

Your standard already does the cross-referencing your framework was built to do: it reads a product against the rules of every market a brand declares, then applies each market's own law by scope. That is the honest, provable version of "benchmarked against international standards," and it is worth stating clearly as positioning, because most Indian brands cannot say it.

What you benchmark against, by market (all current as of mid-2026):

| Market | Framework you cross-reference |
|---|---|
| India (home market, always in scope) | Cosmetics Rules 2020, plus BIS standards including IS 4707 (Parts 1 and 2) |
| European Union | Regulation (EC) 1223/2009: Responsible Person, Product Information File, safety report, CPNP notification. The EU prohibits well over 1,600 substances |
| United States | MoCRA 2022: mandatory facility registration and biennial renewal, product listing, adverse-event reporting, safety substantiation |
| United Kingdom | UK Cosmetics Regulation and SCPN notification, a separate regime since Brexit |
| Canada | Food and Drugs Act, the Cosmetic Regulations, and the Cosmetic Ingredient Hotlist |
| Australia | Industrial Chemicals Act and AICIS |
| ASEAN | ASEAN Cosmetic Directive, as implemented by each member state |

On top of the market laws, your Live Standards Register already tracks the technical standards families: ISO cosmetic GMP, stability, preservation and microbiology; the ISO sunscreen test methods; OECD safety test guidelines; IFRA fragrance standards; SCCS safety guidance; and the ISO environmental-claims standards. That register is already public on the site, and it is the proof of the benchmarking claim. That is what turns it from marketing into something you can defend.

Two cautions, both consistent with the rest of this plan:

1. Say "benchmarked against the major international frameworks," not "against all standards." "All" cannot be substantiated and invites a challenge you would lose. Name what you actually cross-reference, and let the Standards Register carry the proof.
2. Benchmarking means you are aware of, and apply, each market's rules by scope. It does not mean merging the single strictest number from every market into one universal rule. That highest-common-denominator approach is exactly what Part C retires, because markets define scope and exposure differently. The correct line is: comply with each declared market's law, then add The Clean Sheet's own transparent, risk-based requirements on top, with the reasoning published.

Make this first-class in the positioning. The register is live, so the claim is already backed today. Add a short "Benchmarked against" strip to the Standard hub and the For-Brands page, each linking straight to the Live Standards Register, so anyone can check the claim in one click.
